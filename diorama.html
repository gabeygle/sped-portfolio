A Visual Anthology Project

Dioramas

Eight stories from Disability Visibility, each translated into a visual diorama and a short reflection on collaboration, contradiction, and connection.

Drawn from Disability Visibility: First-Person Stories from the Twenty-First Century, edited by Alice Wong (Vintage Books, 2020).

Being Becoming Doing Connecting References
Part One

Being

Unspeakable Conversations

Unspeakable Conversations

Harriet McBryde Johnson

Harriet McBryde Johnson is an attorney and activist from North Carolina. She engaged in debate with the famed Peter Singer, author of Animal Liberation, who has garnered notoriety from two arguments:

  1. Animal consumption is morally impermissible.
  2. Abortion and infanticide of disabled children is morally permissible.

Both arguments are about the nature of suffering and use utilitarian and consequentialist evaluations. Singer’s arguments gain special attention because of the apparent contradiction between the value of animals and the value of disabled people in his paradigm.

Singer and Johnson are both atheists who use secular philosophy to rationalize their arguments; yet they arrive at different conclusions. Johnson’s story describes not only the rational contradictions that arose in the debate but also the relational contradictions that surface when a disabled person and a secular, maximalist utilitarian engage in “polite” dialogue. The diorama renders those contradictions — and the strange “collaboration” that occurred between political enemies.

For Ki’tay D. Davidson, Who Loves Us

For Ki’tay D. Davidson, Who Loves Us

Talila A. Lewis

Where Singer and Johnson’s story exposes the contradictions disabled people may encounter within professional, collaborative relationships, Lewis’s (2020) eulogy for Ki’tay D. Davidson highlights the remarkable harmony and consistency he displayed during his life. Lewis praises Ki’tay’s wisdom and kindness for everyone in the world, comparing him to Martin Luther King Jr., the Dalai Lama, and Paulo Coelho. She deduces a revolutionary spirituality from Ki’tay’s life and encourages the audience to seek hope and love. Her narrative resolves Johnson’s contradictory entanglement with Singer.

“…it was Ki’tay’s ability to sit with and actively love oppressors and those who were violent toward him and marginalized communities—most often solely because they do not adhere to that which has been deemed ‘normal’—that truly set him apart. He could breathe life and love into even those people within mere moments of making their acquaintance. This is what makes him special—reminiscent of what some here may call a prophet; others, a wise man; others still, a light or sage.” — Lewis (2020), in Wong’s Disability Visibility, p. 29
Part Two

Becoming

We Can’t Go Back

We Can’t Go Back

Ricardo T. Thornton Sr.

Ricardo T. Thornton Sr. gave testimony to the United States Senate Health, Education, Labor and Pensions (HELP) Committee in 2012. At the time, the chairman of the committee was Tom Harkin, credited as the primary Senate architect of the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990. Senator Harkin’s brother is deaf.

Thornton’s (2012) original testimony begins, “Good day, Senator Harkin and members of the Senate HELP Committee.” Wong’s (2020) edit in Disability Visibility removes the acknowledgement to Harkin and the committee. This slight edit, made in favor of centering disabled voices, mystifies the history and essential context of Thornton’s testimony as much as a textbook that excludes Thornton entirely.

The point of this class is collaboration. Thornton’s story illustrates how a white man with a deaf brother collaborated with a formerly institutionalized Black man to uphold ADA enforcement of the 1999 Supreme Court Olmstead decision to end the segregation of disabled people. All members of the HELP Committee in 2012 were white, including well-known figures such as John McCain, Lisa Murkowski, Bernie Sanders, and Rand Paul.

Guide Dogs Don’t Lead Blind People. We Wander as One.

Guide Dogs Don’t Lead Blind People. We Wander as One.

Haben Girma

Haben Girma is the first Deafblind person to graduate from Harvard Law School. Her narrative about guide dogs corrects a long-standing ableist misconception that blind people are simply “led” by their guide dogs. Her story shows how the relationship between people and service animals is genuinely complex — a partnership rather than a one-way dependency. This visual diorama illustrates an analogy for that story.

Part Three

Doing

Why My Novel Is Dedicated to My Disabled Friend Maddy

Why My Novel Is Dedicated to My Disabled Friend Maddy

A. H. Reaume

A. H. Reaume is an author who suffered a traumatic brain injury that made it difficult for her to continue writing. She met another woman, Maddy, who also lived with a traumatic brain injury. Reaume hired Maddy to help her complete the book, and the two were able to finish it because of their disabilities, not in spite of them.

The social model of disability suggests that society is the disabling condition, not biology. Reaume and Maddy are a living example of that argument, demonstrating how disabled friendships generate collaborative fruits. I’ve represented this visually with the analogy of two minds pouring together into a single creative act.

Lost Cause

Lost Cause

Reyma McCoy McDeid

In “Lost Cause,” McCoy McDeid (2020) describes her childhood experience of being called a “lost cause” by the people who parented her. Throughout her life she journeys through a series of so-called “lost causes” until she arrives at the Central Iowa Center for Independent Living (CICIL). She is the first person with developmental disabilities to sit on the National Council on Independent Living’s executive board, and she credits her success to her neurodiversity.

According to ProPublica’s nonprofit database (2016), McCoy McDeid served as executive director of CICIL beginning in 2016 and held that position until 2019.

Part Four

Connecting

The Fearless Benjamin Lay: Activist, Abolitionist, Dwarf Person

The Fearless Benjamin Lay

Eugene Grant

Eugene Grant’s narrative of “The Fearless Benjamin Lay” highlights Lay’s revolutionary spirit. Grant reports that Benjamin Lay once disrupted a group of slave owners by spilling red fluid from a bladder all over them; the diorama demonstrates these heroics.

Grant describes how dwarf people are important connectors in human ecology: because Benjamin Lay understood oppression, he was able to build a bridge between himself and enslaved persons. Grant also insists that historians should fearlessly recount dwarfism, because it is an essential element of Lay’s story — erasing it would erase Lay himself.

May The Fearless Benjamin Lay never be forgotten.

Love Means Never Having to Say…Anything

Love Means Never Having to Say…Anything

Jamison Hill

One of the central narratives of Wong’s anthology is that disability is not naturally disabling — disability is a social product. Jamison Hill demonstrates this concept through romance. Once an avid bodybuilder, Hill falls ill with a rare disease and becomes bedridden and unable to speak. He meets a woman who shares his condition, and they fall in love.

She lives thousands of miles away, and they can rarely see each other — only when their health allows. The story demonstrates that disability does not separate these two individuals; rather, it connects them to each other.
